# Ray-marching LOLA horizons and Horizons ephemerides to rank polar sites

Compute visibility the way the published studies do. Take a LOLA polar DEM horizon (20 m near field, coarser grids out to about 300 km, with curvature), compare it with JPL Horizons Sun and Earth directions treated as finite discs, and report the disc convention and mast height next to every percentage. The best-documented sunlight site is the Shackleton–de Gerlache Connecting Ridge (about 88–92% sunlit at 2 m, 95.65% at 10 m, longest dark spells of a few days). The best-documented Earth-facing site is Malapert Massif (about 74% sunlit, Earth in view almost continuously). No source in the notes combines the two into numbers, so the team must compute the combined ranking itself and say so. Mast height moves results more than site choice at the margin. In Barker et al., going from 1 m to 5 m shortens the worst-case shadow at the Connecting Ridge from 13–22 days to 4–8 days ([Barker et al. 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf)). The preset Shackleton point at -89.9, 129.8 lies inside the crater on its wall, so 0% sunlit is the correct answer there, not a bug. Several figures the team might want to quote (Sun and Earth geometry amplitudes, per-site Earth percentages, best calendar dates) have no source in the research and are listed under known gaps.

## Terrain: the horizon is a near-field plus far-field problem

Use PGDA product 90, the 20 m/pixel south-polar LDEM (80–90°S, polar stereographic, MOON_ME frame of DE421, heights above a 1737.4 km sphere), described in Barker et al. 2023 (PSJ 4, 183) ([PGDA 90](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90); [Barker et al. 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230014419/downloads/BarkernewviewSTIupdated.pdf)). It comes with per-pixel height-error (ERR), slope-error and effective-resolution (EFFRES) maps. Poleward of 84°S the effective resolution is about 20 m, but only 15–75% of 20 m pixels contain a LOLA spot, so small features are interpolated rather than measured. The team tested the server and found that it supports HTTP range requests, so windowed reads of the 2.7 GB BigTIFF work. The same test found no CORS header, so fetching from a browser needs a proxy or pre-cut tiles.

Published horizon calculations use nested grids. Mazarico 2011 used 5 m under 5 km, 80 m out to 100 km and 240 m beyond. Barker 2023 used 20 m to 7 km and 480 m out to 310 km. Both took 720 azimuth rays at 0.5° spacing ([Barker 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf)). The decisive terrain physics is that the Sun never rises more than about 1.5° above the horizon, so distant ridges cast shadows. SVS 4893 shows shadows from peaks up to about 120 km away ([NASA SVS 4893](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/4893/)). Curvature must be included. The researchers' arithmetic gives a drop of d²/2R of 2.88 km at 100 km and 25.9 km at 300 km, and a feature of height h at distance d rises to about h/d − d/2R radians. Ignoring curvature overestimates how far a ridge reaches: a 1 km ridge gives 1.5° at about 29 km with curvature, versus 38 km without. This is derived arithmetic, not a published criterion.

DEM error matters mostly for low masts at close range. Clone-based error studies show that metre-scale height errors change illumination materially at 1 m mast height and much less at 5 m. Perturbations usually raise the horizon, so error-inclusive results are less favourable than the nominal DEM ([Barker 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf)). The researchers' rule of thumb is that a 2 m error is 1.1° at 100 m range, 0.23° at 500 m and 0.057° at 2 km. This is their own arithmetic, not a published threshold. The team can treat mid- and far-field horizons beyond about 1 km as sub-0.1° reliable, and near-field horizons at 1 m mast height as the weak point.

QuickMap and Moon Trek are not terrain sources for computation. QuickMap is a JavaScript viewer with LOLA-derived layers (Sun and sky visibility at 60–240 m, slopes, roughness) and no documented API, and scraping its tile service would be undocumented and of unclear terms. Moon Trek exposes OGC WMTS tiles with open CORS on the endpoints tested, but the layer tested was colour-shaded relief, which encodes colour rather than metres. Use them as visual cross-checks and for the team's story, and take numbers from PGDA GeoTIFFs ([Trek WMTS documentation](https://trek.nasa.gov/tiles/apidoc/trekAPI.html?body=moon); [LROC QuickMap 2020 layer list](https://files.actgate.com/quickmap/LROC_QuickMap_Jan2020.pdf)). For attribution, PGDA asks users to cite Barker et al. 2023 and the data DOI 10.60903/gsfcpgda-lola-spole. NASA content is generally not copyrighted in the US, but the page gives no explicit licence ([NASA media guidelines](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/)).

## Ephemerides: Horizons works for the Moon, with traps

Horizons accepts a lunar surface observer as CENTER='coord@301' with COORD_TYPE='GEODETIC' and SITE_COORD as east-positive longitude, latitude and altitude in km. QUANTITIES=4 returns airless apparent azimuth (clockwise from north) and elevation of the target centre, and QUANTITIES=13 returns angular diameter ([Horizons API](https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/doc/horizons.html); [Horizons manual](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/manual.html)). A live test on 14 Nov 2026 showed the service using DE441 and the "MEAN_ME (high precision)" orientation. At -89.54°, 0°E on 2026-11-15 00:00 UT, the Sun was at azimuth 114.44°, elevation +1.366° and angular diameter 1941.6 arcsec. Earth was at azimuth 357.38°, elevation -3.09°, angular diameter 6500.1 arcsec and distance about 404,800 km (live test by the researchers). Three practical constraints apply. Elevation is measured from the tangent plane of the 1737.4 km reference sphere, so terrain masking must be done separately and a DEM site must pass its altitude relative to that sphere. The API sends no CORS header, so a server-side proxy with caching is needed. TLIST accepts up to 10,000 times per request, and no rate limit is published, so the researchers advise sequential, cached requests.

The frame question looks scary but is small. PGDA uses MOON_ME (DE421), and NAIF defines the DE440 ME frame to stay aligned with it: the maximum rotation difference is about 53 cm on the surface over 2000–2040 and slightly over 1 m over 1900–2051 ([NAIF moon_de440 frame kernel](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/generic_kernels/fk/satellites/moon_de440_250416.tf)). The real danger is mixing in the principal-axis frame, which differs from ME by about 875 m (0.029°), or letting Horizons fall back to the low-precision IAU model, which differs by up to about 155 m. The researchers infer that Horizons' MEAN_ME equals the NAIF alias, which Horizons does not state. A SPICE route (naif0012, de440, moon_pa_de440, moon_de440 frame kernel, MOON_ME, LT+S) should reproduce Horizons. The researchers did not run it, so use 3–5 Horizons rows as the check.

Earth and Sun disc size drive the conventions. The Sun's angular radius is about 0.27° (0.2697° from the live 1941.6 arcsec value) and Earth's is about 0.90–1.03° over its distance range. The 1.03° is the researchers' own calculation at perigee. Libration gives Earth an apparent latitude excursion of roughly ±6.7° and a longitude excursion of roughly ±7.9°. Wikipedia quotes maxima of 6°50′ and 7°54′ ([Wikipedia: Libration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libration)), and NASA says Earth "bobs up and down" near 0° longitude ([NASA SVS 5228](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5228)). Because the Sun and Earth sit within a few degrees of the horizon, the 0.27° and about 1° disc radii are the same size as the quantities being measured, and the convention changes the answer.

## Conventions differ between studies, so state yours

The published conventions are only partly stated. PGDA's Earth-visibility maps count Earth visible "if any part of its disc is not obscured" and note this "may be very slightly optimistic for radio link studies that would involve only DSN stations" ([PGDA 69](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69)). Mazarico 2011 modelled the Sun as an extended limb-darkened disc and approximated the visible fraction by cutting the disc at the horizon elevation at the disc-centre azimuth ([Barker 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf)). Barker 2021 used a limb-darkened extended source with 1 h steps. A SPICE-based illumination toolkit used 100 Sun sub-sources and a 671-element Earth ([PMC6398960](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6398960)). No source states a formal sunrise definition or a DSN elevation mask. The JPL study of Bryant (IPN 42-176) obtained 92% average solar availability and 51% direct-to-Earth availability for the best south-pole site over 2009–2028, from a 40 m GSSR DEM. Only its abstract was read, and its disc treatment is unknown ([IPN PR 42-176C](https://ipnpr.jpl.nasa.gov/progress_report/42-176/176C.html)).

## Validation: reproduce the long-run numbers, then the patterns

Validation should be layered. First, check ephemerides against 3–5 Horizons rows and, if possible, a SPICE run. Second, reproduce the long-run published statistics, which require a full 18.6-year cycle and at least hourly steps. The usable benchmarks are in the table. Third, compare qualitative behaviour for short windows, because 15–60 day runs cannot reproduce multi-year percentages.

| Benchmark | Published value | Settings and caveats |
|---|---|---|
| Connecting Ridge, Gläser 2014 | 92.27% at 2 m, 95.65% at 10 m; longest dark typically 3–5 days | About 19 years, DLR abstract summary ([DLR record](https://openpolar.no/Record/ftdlr:oai:elib.dlr.de:94242/Map)). Primary text not read |
| Best Shackleton-rim point, Mazarico 2011 | Sunlit about 240 days per year; longest total darkness about 1.5 days | 240 m DEMs, 6 h steps, several cycles ([NTRS 20120010094](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20120010094)) |
| 20 m LOLA DTMs, 50×50 km areas, 2 m panels | Surface averages 69.5–82.9%; with 2 m panels 77.1–88.0%; best south value 88.0% on the Shackleton–de Gerlache ridge | 1 Jan 2017 to 1 Jan 2037, 1 h steps ([NTRS 20170007365](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/archive/nasa/casi.ntrs.nasa.gov/20170007365.pdf)) |
| Connecting Ridge, Barker 2021, 5 m mast | Average illumination 83–89% at the 1st percentile; longest shadow 4–8 days at the 99th percentile | 5 m LDEMs with 100 error clones, 1 h steps ([Barker 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf)) |
| Empirical check, Speyerer and Robinson 2013 | 3,807 LROC WAC images, one lunar year | Percentage and time-weighted maps; site percentages not on the product pages ([LROC 991](https://lroc.im-ldi.com/images/991)) |

Two cautions apply to these benchmarks. The 20 m DTM study is attributed in the notes both to Gläser 2018 (PSS 162, 170) and to Mazarico, Speyerer, Robinson et al. (NTRS 20170007365), and a secondary source gives 92.1% rather than 88.0% for the same ridge. The two are not reconciled, so cite the NTRS document and treat the other as unverified. The Shackleton interior is also a poor benchmark for sunlit percentages, since the floor and inner wall are permanently shadowed and the preset falls there (see below). The team should also remember that results depend on mast height, grid, step, record length and disc treatment, so a mismatch of a few points need not be an error. In the notes' quantified cases, mast height has the largest effect, 3.4 points between 2 m and 10 m at the ridge, and DEM error is small by comparison (0.3–0.5 m RMS at 5 m grid). No paper directly quantifies solar-disc, azimuth-sampling or far-field-cutoff effects. For Earth visibility, the sanity checks are qualitative: Malapert should show Earth in view near-continuously ([LOLA Malapert post](https://lunarnetworks.blogspot.com/2010/06/lolas-malapert-region.html)), and true polar sites should show Earth below the horizon for long stretches.

## Which sites and dates look best

Sunlight points to the ridge and rim near Shackleton; communications point to Malapert; and no source lets the team call a single winner. The published point values for a 2020 lunar year (Wikipedia, citing an underlying study with unstated mast height and DEM) show Shackleton ridge A (89.68°S, 166.0°W) at 81% mean, Peak near Shackleton B (89.44°S, 141.8°W) at 82%, de Gerlache ridge C (88.71°S, 68.7°W) at 85%, Peak near Shackleton D (88.79°S, 124.5°E) at 86%, and Malapert M1 and M2 at 74% ([Wikipedia: Peak of eternal light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_of_eternal_light)). These are tertiary figures, so verify before presenting them. The daily spread is large: Shackleton ridge A ranges 44–98% across lunar days and Malapert M1 54–95%, so monthly cycles matter. NASA's nine remaining Artemis III regions (Oct 2024) are Peak near Cabeus B, Haworth, Malapert Massif, Mons Mouton Plateau, Mons Mouton, Nobile Rim 1, Nobile Rim 2, de Gerlache Rim 2 and Slater Plain, chosen for terrain safety, Earth visibility and illumination robustness ([NASA release](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-artemis-iii-moon-landing-regions/); [NTRS 20250011660](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250011660)). They are a useful proxy but not an active plan: the 27 Feb 2026 restructure made Artemis III an Earth-orbit test and moved landings to Artemis IV and V in 2028 ([SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/nasa-makes-a-course-correction-for-the-artemis-program/)). NASA gives no coordinates for the regions and publishes no Artemis IV region set.

On dates, the only sourced statements are that polar illumination shifts about 2–3 weeks per year ([NTRS 20250011660](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250011660)) and that Barker's results show higher illumination in southern summer. The researchers read that as favouring the local-summer half of the Sun's annual excursion. This is their inference, not a stated rule. Because the Artemis landings are in 2028, the relevant geometry is 2028 and later, and no source supplies date-specific windows or the 18.6-year cycle phase. The team should therefore compute its own windows for chosen dates and present them as outputs, not published results. Near-term real missions give useful context: Griffin-1 to Mons Mouton (NET Nov 2026, status unchecked since 15 Sept 2026), Blue Moon MK1 and VIPER near Shackleton (2027), IM-4 at Mons Mouton (2027) and Chang'e-7 at the Shackleton rim (slipped to 2027) ([Aerospace America](https://aerospaceamerica.aiaa.org/nasa-expects-one-lunar-launch-this-year-moon-base-manager-says/); [Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/chinas-change-7-moonshot-will-seek-water-ice-at-the-lunar-south-pole)). These dates may have changed. IM-2 landed on the Mons Mouton plateau on 6 Mar 2025, tipped inside a crater and ended within a day, a reminder that terrain safety can dominate site quality ([SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/im-2-mission-ends-one-day-after-lunar-landing/)).

## What our calculator should do

The following are the report's recommendations, derived from the sources above. They are design choices the team can defend, not published standards.

**Earth: disc, with both bounds reported.** Treat Earth as a disc of about 0.9–1.0° radius from the Horizons angular diameter. Report the PGDA-style "any part visible" fraction as the optimistic bound and a centre-above-mask or whole-disc-clear fraction as the conservative bound, because no source gives a DSN mask or says centre versus limb. Label the optimistic figure as comparable to PGDA.

**Sun: disc, not centre.** Compute the visible fraction of a 0.27° radius disc (ideally limb-darkened, using 1941.6 arcsec style Horizons diameters per timestep). For binary "powered" windows, state the rule explicitly and use the same one throughout. The practical default is any part of the disc visible for sunlit and the full disc clear for the conservative bound. A centre-only rule shifts the threshold by 0.27° on a Sun that moves only about 0.0037°/h in elevation near its extreme, which can change percentages noticeably.

**Mast height: a user input with 2 m and 10 m presets, and 1 and 5 m as stress tests.** Never publish a percentage without it. 2 m matches the Gläser and 20 m DTM studies and 10 m matches Gläser 2014 and Mazarico 2011. Barker uses 1 m and 5 m.

**Far-field radius: at least 120 km, ideally about 300 km.** The documented schemes reach 100–310 km. Use 20 m to 5–7 km, 80–120 m to 100 km and 240–480 m to 300 km (the researchers' derivation from the nested schemes). The 80°S edge of the 20 m product is about 304 km from the pole, so beyond that use the 60°S 60/240 m products. Always include d²/2R curvature.

**Grid resolution: 20 m PGDA product 90 near field.** Sample azimuth at 0.5° or finer, since 1° is 175 m at 10 km and can miss narrow ridges. Use hourly or finer time steps for comparison with Gläser and Barker, and state the step because duration quantisation is 1–6 h across the literature.

**Ephemeris and frame: Horizons with CENTER='coord@301' (DE441), cached, via a server proxy, in MOON_ME.** Do not mix in principal-axis coordinates. Pass site altitude relative to the 1737.4 km sphere. Keep a SPICE cross-check if time allows.

**Presets: coordinates must be verified against the DEM.** Shackleton's centre is -89.67, 129.78 (USGS/IAU), diameter about 20.9 km, depth about 4.2 km. By the researchers' geometry the centre is about 10 km from the pole and the crater radius is about 10.5 km, so -89.9, 129.8 is about 7 km from centre toward the pole, roughly 3.5 km inside the rim, on the inner wall. The tool's own elevation of -339 m and horizon of 4.4–33° agree. Label that preset as a crater-wall demonstration where 0% sunlit is expected, and do not use it as a rim benchmark. For rim or ridge tests use published ridge or rim coordinates (for example the Wikipedia table points) after checking DEM elevation. Add Malapert M1 (86.04°S, 2.7°E) as the Earth-visibility case. Show the DEM's height-error maps and warn when a site sits within a few hundred metres of a steep feature at low mast heights.

**Short-window outputs: label them clearly.** A 15–60 day run cannot be compared with 18.6-year percentages. Compare patterns (long lit and dark runs, monthly range) instead, and offer a full-cycle batch mode for the benchmarks.

## Known gaps and uncertainties

The researchers could not access most primary papers in full, and several figures here come from abstracts, search summaries or tertiary pages. The Gläser 2014 and Gläser 2017 numbers (including the 92.1% ridge and 85.5% Shackleton-rim values) and the 2020 point table were not checked against full texts, and Mazarico 2011 was read only as an abstract. The 88.0% versus 92.1% discrepancy for the same ridge is unreconciled. Mast heights and date ranges for the Gläser 2017 values and for the Wikipedia table are unknown, and a related claim that Malapert is less than fully sunlit 11% of the time comes from one unnamed study, set against an IAC-19 abstract claiming more than 300 days of light per year.

No source in the notes quantifies Earth visibility per site: there are no percentages or longest outages for Malapert, Haworth, Faustini, Nobile, Leibnitz Beta, Mons Mouton, Peak near Cabeus B or Slater Plain. The likely sources are Mazarico 2011, Bussey and De Rosa 2012, Kumari et al. 2022 and a 2025 JGR Planets paper (doi 10.1029/2025JE009127), none of which were retrieved. The Bryant 51% direct-to-Earth figure is abstract-only. The Sun's maximum elevation of about 1.5° and the Earth libration amplitudes of ±6.7° and ±7.9° are consistent with the live Horizons result (+1.37° Sun elevation) and the Wikipedia libration figures, but the 18.6-year cycle phase, the 27.2/27.55-day periods, the 356,000–406,700 km distance range and the ±1.7% solar-diameter variation are unsourced background knowledge. No source gives a sunrise definition for the major papers, a DSN elevation mask or LunaNet requirements, or Horizons' own accuracy for lunar az/el.

Several terrain and tool details are unverified. The PGDA file's COG tiling, nodata value and data type were not probed; the 30,400 × 30,400 pixel size is the researchers' arithmetic. Whether MEAN_ME equals the NAIF MOON_ME_DE440_ME421 alias is inferred. No direct sensitivity study of horizon error against pixel size, and no study comparing independent calculators, was found. Neither QuickMap nor Moon Trek has retrieved terms of use or a current layer inventory, and the exact Moon Trek south-polar layer identifiers were not found. Licensing for PGDA data redistribution is unstated, so legal review is advisable for anything beyond a hackathon. Mission schedules are fast-moving: Griffin-1's operator and launch status, the Blue Origin delay accounts (two sources disagree on the May 2026 events), the Chang'e-7 date and the Artemis IV region set were not confirmed as of 14 Nov 2026. Coordinates for the Artemis regions, Mazarico's numbered sites and several CLPS landing sites were not retrieved. Thermal survival and battery-sizing figures, and relay coverage (Lunar Pathfinder slipped to 2027 or later; Queqiao-2 not covered), are also unsourced, so any statement that a Shackleton-ridge lander needs a relay is the researchers' inference only.

## Conclusion

The team's strongest defence is procedural, not numerical. Every number it shows should carry its mast height, disc rule, grid, step and date range, because the literature's headline percentages differ by more than their stated errors for exactly these reasons. The sourced results show that mast height is the dominant lever, that near-pole Sun and Earth sit at the same angular scale as their own discs, and that frame errors are negligible next to the DEM as long as PA coordinates are not mixed in.

The open scientific question for the project is the combined ranking: sunlight leaders such as the Connecting Ridge sit near 89.5°S where Earth is borderline, while Malapert is Earth-facing but sunlit only about 74% of the time. Running both through one calculator, with the Earth disc bounds and several mast heights, is the original contribution the team can make and defend. They should present results as computed estimates validated against the benchmark table, and keep the unverified items in the gaps section out of headline claims.
