# Closing the five lunar data gaps with free tools

None of the five gaps is unfixable, but only two are fully solved today. Higher-resolution elevation is **solvable now**: LOLA LDEM_256 exists as four hemisphere tiles of 2,123,366,400 bytes each, readable with HTTP Range exactly like LDEM_128, and near the pole 5 m, 10 m and 20 m polar grids exist as Cloud Optimized GeoTIFFs. Landing-safety data is **solvable with caveats**. Slope can be computed from the polar DEMs, and the only NASA threshold the notes confirm is a landing slope under 10 degrees over 100 m. No polar rock or hazard layer exists, because Diviner rock abundance stops near 60 degrees latitude. Programmatic use of Moon Trek is **solvable with caveats**: its ArcGIS ImageServer `identify` query returned metric LOLA values with open CORS, while QuickMap stays a link-only target. Independent validation is **solvable with caveats**: the published figures are independent of PGDA product 69 but sit in papers, and none was found as a downloadable per-site table. Testing the optional Claude explanation feature is **solvable now** with mocks and fixtures, at about 17 cents per 1,000 live calls if a key is ever used. Several earlier claims need correcting. LDEM_256 exists. Moon Trek tiles are 8-bit images, not metres. PGDA product 69 shares the LOLA terrain family, so it checks the calculation but not the terrain.

## Corrections to earlier claims

The first correction concerns elevation. The earlier finding that LDEM_256 does not exist is wrong. The name `LDEM_256.IMG` returns 404, but the imbrium directory lists four 256 ppd files of 2,123,366,400 bytes each: `LDEM_256_00N_90N_000_180.IMG`, `LDEM_256_00N_90N_180_360.IMG`, `LDEM_256_90S_00S_000_180.IMG` and `LDEM_256_90S_00S_180_360.IMG`. The test showed the 404 came only from the wrong file name ([LOLA IMG directory](https://imbrium.mit.edu/DATA/LOLA_GDR/CYLINDRICAL/IMG/)). The label of the south-west tile gives 23,040 lines by 46,080 samples, scaling factor 0.5 and offset 1,737,400 ([label](https://imbrium.mit.edu/DATA/LOLA_GDR/CYLINDRICAL/IMG/LDEM_256_90S_00S_000_180.LBL)).

The second item is a correction to this report's own first draft. The researchers could not confirm the citation behind the 20 m polar terrain and flagged "Barker et al. 2023, PSJ 4, 183" as unconfirmed. **It is correct.** NASA's PGDA product 90 page says: "Please cite this paper when using these products: Barker et al., 2023 Planet. Sci. J., 4, 183. https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/acf3e1", with data DOI 10.60903/gsfcpgda-lola-spole (checked directly on [PGDA 90](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90) on 2026-11-15). Barker et al. 2021 (Planetary and Space Science 203, 105119, doi 10.1016/j.pss.2020.105119) is a different, earlier paper on illumination error estimates and should be cited separately where used ([NTRS 20205009660](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf)).

The third concerns validation. PGDA product 69 is the Mazarico et al. 2011 LOLA product, so comparing the app with it tests the geometry and disc conventions but not the terrain ([PGDA 69](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69)). A match with product 69 is therefore not independent evidence about the terrain.

The fourth concerns Moon Trek. Its WMTS layers are 8-bit gray or colour PNGs. A sample tile of `LRO_LOLA_DEM_Global_128ppd_v04` was 256 by 256, 8-bit gray plus alpha, so decoded heights would be quantised into about 256 levels ([Trek WMTS API](https://trek.nasa.gov/tiles/apidoc/trekAPI.html?body=moon)). The 80 m step size is the notes' own inference from a roughly 20 km range. The metric path is separate: the ArcGIS ImageServer `identify` call for `LRO_LOLA_DEM_Global_256ppd_v06` returned metric values, but exporting a window of pixels from that service was not tested.

The upgrade from LDEM_128 to LDEM_256 is one change in the proxy: pick the tile from latitude and longitude, then apply the offset formula. One catch is the equirectangular geometry. A 50 km window at the equator is about 424 columns by 424 rows, but at 85 S it spans about 19 degrees of longitude, roughly 4,900 columns, and about 4.1 MB. That arithmetic is the notes' inference. For windows poleward of about 85 S, use the polar grids instead.

The 20 m PGDA file is the same family the app already uses. The notes measured a mean compressed tile of 596,190 B near its centre, which puts a 50 km window at 20 m (2,500 px, 5 to 6 tiles per side) at roughly 15 to 21 MB, an estimate. For the 5 m COG the notes measured about 657 KB per tile, so a 2 km window of 400 px costs 0.7 to 2.6 MB and a full 50 km window would cost about 263 MB, which is too heavy for a browser. The 87S README describes 97 fields of 20 x 20 km blended with 30 to 55 cm typical residuals ([README folder](https://asc-pds-services.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/mosaic/Lunar_safed/LMAP/SouthPolar/)). Barker et al. 2021 report median height RMS error of 0.30 to 0.50 m, slope error 1.5 to 2.5 degrees, and about 90% of 5 m pixels interpolated, so 5 m is finer in posting than in measured content ([Barker 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2020.105119)). The notes warn that Barker states shorter-baseline products can show orbit-determination artifacts, which is a reason to keep 20 m as the default reference, but that sentence came from a search snippet and counts as partial.

### What was not established

Four items stay unconfirmed. First, whether the 87S COG has internal overviews (the notes inferred a 200 km extent from 40000 px x 5 m). Second, Range and CORS behaviour of Zenodo and of the 10 m COG. Third, whether JP2 files can be partially decoded in a browser; no JP2 decoder was tested. Fourth, no window was ever decoded end to end through a client, so every "bytes per window" figure is arithmetic from verified dimensions, apart from the measured tile sizes. LROC NAC DTMs (1 to 5 m, per stereo pair) sit on `pds.mcp.nasa.gov` with Range and CORS `*`, tested on the Apollo 16 file of 558,786,082 B, but the TIF's internal tiling was not parsed and no IM-1 (Malapert A) product was found ([LROC product page](https://data.lroc.im-ldi.com/lroc/view_rdr_product/NAC_DTM_APOLLO16_1_M119747321_50CM)).

### Implementation and effort

Step one, about half a day: add the four LDEM_256 tile names to the proxy, switch the tile by latitude and longitude, and keep LDEM_128 as fallback. Step two, about one day: add the 20 m PGDA COG (already in use) as the default polar source and make the 5 m 87S COG an optional zoom layer, read directly from the browser with a COG library, since it has CORS. The library choice (geotiff.js was suggested in the notes) is untested here. Step three, one to two days, optional: bake small clips of the 13 Artemis regions from Zenodo offline into compact tiles. The effort figures are estimates for this app, not sourced facts. Verdict: **solvable now** for 118 m global and 20 m polar, **solvable with caveats** for 5 m (interpolation, size, 87 to 90 S only for the CORS-open file) and for NAC DTMs (per site, patchy).

## Landing safety: slope is computable, rocks are not

### Slope and the one confirmed threshold

PGDA product 78 supplies 5 m LDEMs with slope, slope-uncertainty and 100 error-clone files for the sites listed above ([PGDA 78](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/78)). Product 104 (Bertone, Barker, Mazarico, Beyer, Petro, PSJ, doi 10.3847/PSJ/ae5b70) adds 5 m shape-from-shading DEMs for the 13 Artemis III regions with slope and roughness maps at multiple baselines, though the page does not list the baselines and the zip contents were not inspected ([PGDA 104](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/104)). The only agency threshold the notes read in full is from NASA HLS slides: land within 6 degrees latitude of the pole, landing slope under 10 degrees evaluated over 100 m, EVA slope under 20 degrees over 2 km, Earth visibility and Sun visibility criteria with mask values of 25% and 5% respectively ([Artemis HLS Update 2024](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240009596/downloads/ArtemisHLSUpdate_2024.pdf)). The slides do not state the DEM baseline behind the 10 degree map.

Other numbers are weaker and must not be presented as standards. An arXiv multi-criteria study buffers pixels under 5 degrees on 5 m LOLA DEMs, per a fetched summary ([arXiv 2406.19863](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19863v2)). The 5 degree figure there is the authors' screening choice and conflicts with NASA's 10 degrees. A press report gives 8 degrees as crew tilt tolerance, secondary only ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/03/11/nasa_oig_artemis_lander/)). Chandrayaan-3 reports slopes under 4 degrees over 78% of its primary area, with the baseline unstated ([arXiv 2308.10712](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.10712)). The notes could not verify slope tolerances for CLPS, IM-1, SLIM or Apollo from primary documents, and found no published table of per-site area fractions below 5 or 10 degrees, so the app computes its own and says so.

### Rocks, roughness, PSR

Diviner rock abundance (Bandfield et al. 2011) is a 32 ppd product covering about 60 S to 60 N, so it does not reach the south pole ([ODE GDR L3](https://ode.rsl.wustl.edu/moon/pagehelp/Content/Missions_Instruments/LRO/DIVINER/GDR_L3.htm)). A newer 128 ppd Diviner mosaic to 70 degrees was seen only as a search snippet, and the 60 versus 70 degree conflict is unresolved. No polar boulder layer was found; LROC NAC boulder maps, Mini-RF and CLPS data products were not opened. The honest app label is "rock abundance not assessed poleward of about 60 degrees". Permanent shadow (LPSR) rasters exist in PGDA 69 at 240, 120 and 60 m (65 to 90, 75 to 90 and 85 to 90 degrees S), with `LPSR_65S_240M_201608.JP2` and `LPSR_75S_120M_201608.JP2` seen in the imbrium listing at `http://imbrium.mit.edu/EXTRAS/ILLUMINATION/JP2/`; the 85S 60 m name and the COG filenames are unverified. `AVGVISIB_85S_060M_201608.TIF` (33,994,464 B) was verified with a 206 Range reply at `https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/MoonIllumination/AVGVISIB_85S_060M_201608.TIF`. No licence is stated on the PGDA pages; the notes treat the data as public NASA data but did not see the terms.

### Implementation and effort

Compute slope from the 20 m DEM in the same window already loaded, report fractions below 5, 10 and 20 degrees, and label the baseline. Show the 10 degrees over 100 m as "from NASA Artemis III briefings", with the others as illustrative. About one day for slope fractions, half a day for an LPSR overlay, and one to two days for per-region summaries from Zenodo, which must be pre-processed offline into small JSON because the zips are 1 to 4 GB. Suggested caveat wording (the notes' own draft): slope is a screening aid at stated baseline, lander-scale slopes are steeper, about 90% of 5 m pixels are interpolated, boulders and small craters are not assessed. Verdict: **solvable with caveats** for slope and PSR; **not solvable** for polar rock and boulder hazards with the public data found.

## Moon Trek works as a data source, QuickMap does not

### Moon Trek

Tile access is documented and CORS-open: the pattern is `https://trek.nasa.gov/tiles/Moon/EQ/<layer>/1.0.0/default/default028mm/{z}/{row}/{col}.png`, with equatorial, north and south polar services and no terms, key or rate limit stated on the API page ([Trek tile API](https://trek.nasa.gov/tiles/apidoc/trekAPI.html?body=moon)). Test results: the WAC mosaic tile `LRO_WAC_Mosaic_Global_303ppd_v02/.../0/0/0.jpg` returned 49,475 B with `Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *`, and the colour-shade capabilities file returned 200. Layer-name guesses failed with 404 and directory listings return 403, so exact names come from `https://trek.nasa.gov/tiles/Moon/rasters/{eq|np|sp}/list.json`; only the `eq` list was fetched, so the south polar layer names and tile matrix set remain to be read.

The metric route is the ArcGIS ImageServer. The request `https://trek.nasa.gov/moon/trekarcgis/rest/services/LRO_LOLA_DEM_Global_256ppd_v06/ImageServer/identify?geometry={"x":0,"y":-89.5}&geometryType=esriGeometryPoint&returnGeometry=false&f=json` returned HTTP 200, CORS `*` and `"value":"-721.5"`; at y = -89.99 it returned `"1274"`. The south-polar service `LRO_LOLA_DEM_SPole45_100mp_v04/ImageServer/identify` with x,y in metres from the pole returned `"1277"` at the pole ([ImageServer metadata](https://trek.nasa.gov/moon/trekarcgis/rest/services/LRO_LOLA_DEM_Global_256ppd_v06/ImageServer?f=json)). Absolute values were not cross-checked against published LOLA numbers, and window export (`exportImage`) was not tested, so single-point lookups are verified while window reads are not. The viewer's config lists many tool services (`elevationProfile`, `light`, `los`, `slope`, `rock/detect`) at `https://trek.nasa.gov/moon/jpl/config/config.json`, but `light` returned 400 on a bare GET, `los` returned 405, and `elevationProfile/calculate` returned a degenerate reply, so no illumination or visibility point query was obtained. The config also lists a Solr-style search that returned 9,391 items for `q=polar`, which could power site bookmarks. Moon Trek cannot be iframed (`frame-ancestors 'self' aelp.smartsparrow.com`), and no permalink grammar was recovered.

### QuickMap

QuickMap blocks framing (`x-frame-options: sameorigin`, `frame-ancestors 'self'`) and the only confirmed query parameter in its bundle is `?fullscreen` ([QuickMap](https://quickmap.lroc.im-ldi.com/)). A hidden footprint API, `https://features-api.quickmap.io/collections/nac.geojson?limit=1&intersects=<minLon>,<minLat>,<maxLon>,<maxLat>`, returned GeoJSON with CORS `*`, and could drive a "NAC coverage here" badge. It is undocumented, with no stated permission, so it carries breakage risk. The tile hosts (`dem-tiles.b-cdn.net`, `lroc-tiles.actgate.com`) could not be turned into working URLs. LROC terms say PDS-delivered data are public domain, require the credit "NASA/GSFC/Arizona State University", and name the WMS Lunaserv as the machine interface, whose endpoint and CORS were not tested ([LROC terms](https://lroc.im-ldi.com/about/terms)). The notes could not retrieve the Space Apps 2026 challenge page, so whether Moon Trek is a sanctioned resource for this challenge is unconfirmed.

### Implementation and effort

Add a cached proxy route for `identify` (half a day) and use it as a cross-check on the app's own DEM height at each site, which also gives the "real data source" story. Add Moon Trek WAC or hillshade tiles as a backdrop after reading the south-polar layer list (half a day to one day). Keep QuickMap as a plain outbound link to the root URL; the notes suggest someone copy a real permalink from each viewer's Share dialog, about ten minutes in a browser, before building site-specific links. Verdict: **solvable with caveats** for Moon Trek (point elevation and tiles verified, window export and illumination services untested); QuickMap is **not solvable** as an embed or documented data source, though the NAC footprint endpoint is usable at your own risk.

## Independent validation: use the papers, not product 69

The correct framing is that PGDA 69 verifies the app's arithmetic against LOLA-derived maps, and independence has to come from other terrain or observations. The independent sources the notes verified by reading are these. Gläser et al. 2014 give Connecting Ridge at 92.3% (2 m) and 95.66% (10 m) over 19 years from a 20 m LOLA DTM, with longest darkness 3 to 5 days ([DLR elib 94242](https://elib.dlr.de/94242)); the Icarus version reports 92.27% and 95.65%, search-only ([openpolar record](https://openpolar.no/Record/ftdlr:oai:elib.dlr.de:94240/Details)). Gläser et al. 2018 give 69.5 to 82.9% for the three best regions per pole, and 77.1 to 88.0% for a 2 m lander, maximal at the Shackleton-de Gerlache ridge ([DLR elib 119091](https://elib.dlr.de/119091)). Barker et al. 2021, Table 2 Site 1, report 1st-percentile average illumination of 67.4 to 71.2% at 1 m and 82.8 to 89.3% at 5 m, with longest continuous illumination 119 to 231 days and 99th percentile longest shadow 4 to 8 days at 5 m, over 2024 to 2026 only ([Barker 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf)). Mazarico et al. 2011 report a Shackleton-rim location sunlit continuously for 240 days per year with darkness up to about 1.5 days ([NTRS 20120010094](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20120010094)). For Earth, Bryant (JPL) found a best south-pole site with 92% solar and 51% direct-to-Earth availability on a 40 m GSSR radar DEM ([IPN 42-176C](https://ipnpr.jpl.nasa.gov/progress_report/42-176/176C.html)), and SpaceOps 2010 repeats it as about 50% ([NTRS 20150008843](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20150008843)).

Three items are search-only and must be labelled that way. Bussey et al. 2010 (Kaguya) report 86% for the best site, with 78% (Kaguya high-res) and 90% (LOLA) from an AGU abstract ([Bussey on ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Icar..208..558B)); the ADS pages were not opened. Speyerer and Robinson 2013 produced observed LROC WAC lit-fraction maps from 15 Feb 2010 to 5 Feb 2011, so one lunar year, independent of any DEM, available as `WAC_POLE_ILL_PCT_SOUTH` ([LROC page](https://data.lroc.im-ldi.com/lroc/view_rdr/WAC_POLE_ILL_PCT_SOUTH)); the file URL, size and licence were not tested and site values were not verified. Qualitative Malapert statements ("Earth always in view", sunlit 90% or more) come from a Lowman outpost study seen only in a search result ([Lowman](https://www3.nd.edu/~cneal/lunar-l/Malapert-Mt-Outpost.pdf)). The notes found no verified per-site Earth percentage for Haworth, Faustini, Nobile or Peak near Shackleton, and no Earth figures from the 2018 Mazarico paper beyond figures ([PMC6398960](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6398960)). The chance of exact matches is small because the studies differ in epoch, mast height, disc rule and terrain; PGDA counts Earth visible if any part of the disc is unobscured, and Mazarico's abstract says 6 h resolution while the PGDA page says hourly, a discrepancy left unresolved.

Mission data points are thin. IM-2 landed 6 March 2025 on its side in a crater, "sunlight could not reach the solar cells", and ended about 12 h 45 min after landing ([SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/im-2-mission-ends-one-day-after-lunar-landing/)). The Chandrayaan-3 landing time (23 Aug 2023 12:33 UTC at about 69.37 S, 32.35 E) is documented ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/chandrayaan-3-makes-historic-touchdown-on-the-moon/)), but the 8.75 degree Sun elevation comes from an unverified forum post and should be rechecked in Horizons.

### Implementation and effort

Build a validation table in the repo with one row per published number: study, epoch, mast height, disc rule, value, and the app's value under matched settings (about one day). Run the app at Connecting Ridge with 2 m and 10 m masts over a 19-year window against Gläser, and at 1 m and 5 m over 2024 to 2026 against Barker; read the 240-day-continuous figure against Mazarico. Add the IM-2 and Chandrayaan-3 timelines as qualitative sanity checks. Download the WAC percentage map only after confirming its file URL. Verdict: **solvable with caveats**: independent numbers exist for Connecting Ridge and the Shackleton rim, none was verified for Earth visibility per site except Bryant's single best-site figure, and exact agreement should not be expected.

## Testing the Claude explanation feature without a key

Mock first. The model should be a replaceable dependency: a mocked `POST /ai/explain`, recorded fixtures labelled as fixtures, contract tests on request and response shapes (200, 400, 429, 500, missing key returning 503 with the button hidden), and unit tests for the rule that rejects any reply containing a number not in the verdict. Adversarial cases for that guard include invented percentages, ranges, thousands separators, decimal forms such as 5.0 versus 5, unicode minus, and digits inside names like Site 3. This plan is engineering recommendation, not sourced research. The app should pass the same end-to-end suite with no key at all.

A live call is cheap if wanted. The notes list `claude-haiku-5-5` at $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens for prompts up to 100k tokens ([Pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing); [Models](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/overview)). That puts a 200-in, 300-out call at about $0.00017, or roughly $0.17 per 1,000 calls. This is the notes' arithmetic and excludes thinking tokens, since Haiku 5.5 defaults to adaptive thinking at medium effort and those tokens bill as output. The notes found no official free tier, student or hackathon credit programme; third-party claims of about $5 trial credit conflict with each other and are unverified. The Claude Startups programme is not accepting applications ([Claude Startups](https://claude.com/programs/startups)). Free alternatives are Ollama locally (REST at `http://localhost:11434/api/chat`, [README](https://github.com/ollama/ollama/blob/main/README.md)) and WebLLM in the browser on WebGPU ([WebLLM](https://webllm.mlc.ai/docs/)); model names, sizes and speeds were not verified, and small models will trip the number guard more often. The Gemini free tier has limits that conflict across secondary sources.

For key safety, keep `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` as a server-only secret ([Vercel env vars](https://vercel.com/docs/environment-variables)); the notes recommend a structured verdict as input, a hard `max_tokens`, per-IP and global daily limits in a shared store, a Console spend limit (not verified), and a fallback to the deterministic brief. For usability, the notes cite Nielsen's heuristics ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/)), five-user testing finding about 85% of problems under a 31% per-user model, with 3 to 4 users per distinct group ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/)), the SUS questionnaire with a 68 average and 80.3+ as an A ([MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/sus/)), and W3C Easy Checks, which are not a full audit ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/easy-checks/)). The task protocol, thresholds and SUS wording in the notes are the researchers' drafts, with SUS item wording recalled, not fetched. Space Apps judges used Impact, Creativity, Validity, Relevance and Presentation in 2022 ([2022 guide](https://spaceapps-prod.s3.amazonaws.com/media/documents/Space_Apps_2022_Judging_and_Awards_Guide.pdf)); the 2026 criteria were not retrieved. Effort: half a day for guard tests and mock server, one day for a three-to-five-person session plus Lighthouse and axe runs. Verdict: **solvable now** for testing, **solvable with caveats** for live use (no free credit confirmed, low marginal cost).

## Conclusion

The old "unfixable" label mostly meant "not yet tested". Every gap now has a concrete path, but the paths differ in kind: elevation and Moon Trek point lookups are engineering tasks, validation and safety are scientific caveats that no download removes, and the AI feature is a testing-discipline question. The sharpest new insight is that independence in validation comes from different terrain, epochs and observation methods, so the right claim is agreement within the spread of published studies rather than a match to PGDA 69.

What remains out of reach with public data is hazard truth: polar rock abundance, lander-scale slopes and boulder maps. The app can screen for slope and shadow at 20 m and 5 m, and should say plainly that it does not certify a landing site.
