# Lunar south-pole landing sites and dates for solar power and direct-to-Earth (DTE) communication

Research date: 14 Nov 2026. Coverage is partial: the search tool returned few primary full texts, so several requested items are listed under Gaps. Items marked "may have changed" come from fast-moving schedule reporting.

## Q1. Which sites are CLPS, Artemis and international targets, with coordinates and rationale?

### Takeaway
NASA's Artemis III region list (13 in 2022, cut to 9 in Oct 2024) is the main named set. Artemis III is no longer a landing mission (Feb 2026 restructure). CLPS south-pole deliveries now centre on Mons Mouton (Griffin-1, NET Nov 2026) and near Shackleton (Blue Moon MK1/VIPER, 2027). Chang'e-7 targets the Shackleton rim but slipped to 2027. NASA pages give no coordinates for the regions.

### Cited Findings
- The original 13 Artemis III regions (2022) are: Connecting Ridge, Connecting Ridge Extended, de Gerlache Rim 1, de Gerlache Rim 2, Haworth, Malapert Massif, Leibnitz Beta Plateau, Nobile Rim 1, Nobile Rim 2, Amundsen Rim, Peak Near Shackleton, Faustini Rim A, de Gerlache-Kocher Massif. NASA's visualization gives no coordinates. — [NASA SVS 5013](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5013)
- Oct 2024 narrowing to 9 regions, in no priority order: Peak near Cabeus B, Haworth, Malapert Massif, Mons Mouton Plateau, Mons Mouton, Nobile Rim 1, Nobile Rim 2, de Gerlache Rim 2, Slater Plain. — [NASA release 28 Oct 2024](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-artemis-iii-moon-landing-regions/)
- Selection factors named by NASA: science potential, launch window availability, terrain suitability, communications with Earth, lighting. Specific sites within regions are chosen only after launch dates are set, because dates determine transfer trajectories and surface conditions. — [NASA release](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-artemis-iii-moon-landing-regions/)
- NASA LPSC 2026 abstract (George, Rubio, Lawrence, Curell), "Downselection From Thirteen to Nine":
  - The cut came from end-to-end availability analysis covering launch opportunities, transit geometry, communications, landing performance, illumination and terrain safety.
  - Terrain safety, Earth visibility and illumination robustness were key.
  - More than one region is kept to maintain annual availability.
  - If Artemis III used DTE, regions with greater Earth visibility were preferred.
  - Operations are from NRHO.
  - Surface stay assumed roughly 5.75–6.25 days.
  - Polar illumination shifts about 2–3 weeks per year.
  - The nine-region set applies only to Artemis III, not Artemis IV and later.
  - The abstract gives no numeric Earth-visibility values or coordinates.
  — [NTRS 20250011660](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250011660)
- An earlier NASA statement said all 13 regions contain sites with continuous sunlight over a 6.5-day stay. Astronomy.com says some rims (de Gerlache, Nobile) are narrow with slopes unsuited to stable landing. Both are secondhand via a search summary. — [Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/space-exploration/here-are-the-nine-sites-where-artemis-3-might-land-on-the-moon/)
- Artemis restructure of 27 Feb 2026:
  - Artemis III becomes an Earth-orbit test with one or both HLS landers, ideally by mid-2027.
  - Artemis IV (early 2028) and Artemis V (late 2028) are the landing missions.
  - No 2028 landing sites were named.
  — [SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/nasa-makes-a-course-correction-for-the-artemis-program/); [CSIS](https://aerospace.csis.org/artemis-rewritten-nasas-new-moon-plan-its-risks-and-whether-the-u-s-can-still-beat-china/)
- Artemis II launched 1 Apr 2026 and splashed down 10 Apr 2026 after a lunar flyby with crew including CSA's Jeremy Hansen. It was not a landing. — [OPB/AP](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/04/10/artemis-ii-astronauts-return-from-moon-with-a-splashdown-to-close-out-a-record-breaking-lunar-voyage)
- CLPS manifest as of Jan 2026 (no coordinates given):
  - IM-1: Malapert A, landed 22 Feb 2024, tipped, about a week of operations.
  - IM-2: south-pole region, landed 6 Mar 2025.
  - Blue Moon Pathfinder: Shackleton Connecting Ridge area.
  - Griffin-1: south pole.
  - IM-4: Mons Mouton, early 2027.
  - Blue Moon MK1 with VIPER: near Shackleton, late 2027.
  - Blue Ghost 2 and APEX 1.0 are far side, not polar.
  — [New Space Economy](https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/01/16/commercial-lunar-payload-services-clps-missions-2024-to-2027/) (the page is internally inconsistent about VIPER's carrier)
- IM-2 (Athena) landed 6 Mar 2025 on the Mons Mouton plateau about 250–400 m from its target and inside a crater. It tipped on its side, ran some payloads briefly (including PRIME-1), lost power and was declared over within a day. Intuitive Machines called it the southernmost lunar landing. Sources differ on the miss distance; an altimeter problem is reported as the cause. — [SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/im-2-mission-ends-one-day-after-lunar-landing/); [Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/space-exploration/intuitive-machines-im-2-moon-lander-is-lying-dead-in-a-crater/)
- NASA awarded Blue Origin a CLPS task order of up to $190M (Sept 2025) to deliver VIPER on a second Blue Moon MK1 in late 2027. The delivery is an option, exercised after the base task and the first MK1 flight. — [SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/viper-might-have-a-new-ride-to-the-moon-on-blue-origin/); [Space.com](https://www.space.com/astronomy/moon/viper-lives-jeff-bezos-blue-origin-will-land-ice-hunting-nasa-rover-on-the-moon-in-2027)
- Status per Aerospace America (15 Sept 2026):
  - Griffin-1 (Voyager Technologies per the article) launches NET November 2026 and lands at Mons Mouton with Astrolab's FLIP rover. It is the only 2026 lunar launch NASA expects.
  - Blue Origin Mark 1 is slated for early 2027. It was delayed by the May 2026 New Glenn pad explosion, and New Glenn remains "Plan A".
  - Intuitive Machines' Nova-C "Trinity" slipped to Q1 2027 and goes to Reiner Gamma.
  - Blue Ghost 2 is later in 2027.
  — [Aerospace America](https://aerospaceamerica.aiaa.org/nasa-expects-one-lunar-launch-this-year-moon-base-manager-says/). May have changed since; check for a Griffin-1 launch announcement.
- Another report says Blue Moon Pathfinder Mission 1 is delayed indefinitely after a New Glenn failure (NG-3, 19 Apr 2026) and a late-May pad event. The two accounts of May differ. — [Space Odyssey Hub](https://spaceodysseyhub.com/moon/landers/blue-moon-pathfinder-mission-1) (low-quality aggregator)
- Chang'e-7:
  - Target is the Shackleton crater rim (crater about 21 km wide), with a flat sunlit spot chosen from orbit. Planetary Society says no final area has been published, and landing accuracy is required to be better than 100 m.
  - It carries a hopper (up to 15 km per hop) and a rover.
  - Launch slipped to 2027, announced 23–24 Aug 2026 (typhoon at Wenchang). The new date is not given.
  — [Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/chinas-change-7-moonshot-will-seek-water-ice-at-the-lunar-south-pole); [Planetary Society](https://www.planetary.org/space-missions/change-7)
- Chandrayaan-4 (sample return) was first reported for the Shiv Shakti Point area (about 69°S). One report instead says 85–90°S. The site is not final. Chandrayaan-5/LUPEX (ISRO–JAXA) was approved by the Indian Cabinet on 10 Mar 2025. — [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/?p=507567) via search summary; [Business Today](https://businesstoday.in/visualstories/news/3-kg-of-lunar-rock-india-aims-for-moons-south-pole-with-chandrayaan-4-sample-return-mission-182640-28-10-2024)

### Inferences
- With Artemis III now in orbit, the nine-region list is not an active landing plan. It is the best public proxy for NASA's illumination and Earth-visibility screening, and the Artemis IV region set is not yet public.
- Mons Mouton is the nearest-term polar landing target (Griffin-1 NET Nov 2026, IM-4 in 2027). Shackleton-area sites follow in 2027 (Blue Moon MK1 and Chang'e-7).

### Gaps
- Coordinates for the Artemis regions: NASA gives none in the pages read.
- Mission status, coordinates and rationale for Luna-25 (crashed Aug 2023), Luna-26, ispace and Firefly were not found in this session. Chandrayaan-3 (Shiv Shakti, about 69°S) is only partly covered.
- VIPER's own target regions (Nobile rim area and others) were not confirmed.
- IM-2's exact landing coordinates and the IM-1 Malapert A coordinates were not retrieved.
- Whether Griffin-1 has launched since 15 Sept 2026 is unknown. The Aerospace America article calls the operator Voyager Technologies; other trackers say Astrobotic, so confirm.
- The "Artemis III finalised at Malapert Massif" claims on mirror domains (hot-sitemirror-store.pages.dev) are unreliable and contradict the NASA record. Ignore them.

## Q2. What do published studies say about illumination, darkness, Earth visibility, and which sites have both?

### Takeaway
The Shackleton–de Gerlache Connecting Ridge has the best published illumination (about 88–92%, longest dark spell a few days at 2–10 m mast height). Malapert massif has a lower, roughly 74%, but is on the Earth-facing side. I found no hard numeric Earth-visibility percentages, so a combined sun-and-Earth ranking is not possible from sourced numbers.

### Cited Findings
- Mazarico et al. 2011 (Icarus 211, LOLA, 240 m/pix DEMs, simulations over several 18.6-yr cycles at 6 h steps, validated against LROC WAC images):
  - A site near the Shackleton rim is sunlit about 240 days per year, with a longest continuous darkness of about 1.5 days.
  - About 10 m of height gain substantially improves average illumination and shortens night.
  - Permanently shadowed area is about 16,055 km² (south) and 12,866 km² (north).
  — [NTRS 20120010094](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20120010094)
- Gläser et al. 2014 (Icarus 243, 78): Connecting Ridge is sunlit 92.27% of the time at 2 m above ground and 95.65% at 10 m, over a one-year analysis. Longest continuous darkness is typically 3–5 days. — [Search summary of DLR record elib.dlr.de/94240](https://elib.dlr.de/94240). Primary text not read, so verify.
- Gläser et al. 2018 (PSS 162, 170, 20 m/pix LOLA DTMs, 50×50 km areas, hourly steps 1 Jan 2017 to 1 Jan 2037 = one 18.6-yr cycle):
  - Three high-illumination regions per pole, with surface averages of 69.5–82.9%.
  - With 2 m solar panels, averages are 77.1–88.0%.
  - The best south-pole value, 88.0%, is on the ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache.
  — [DLR elib 119091](https://elib.dlr.de/119091). A secondary source gives 92.1% for the same ridge; the two are not reconciled.
- Barker et al. 2020/21 (PSS 105119, new LOLA-only 5 m/pix LDEMs of 4 sites, 100 error "clones", Jan 2024 to Jan 2026, 1 h steps, Sun as limb-darkened extended source):
  - Site 1 is the Connecting Ridge, Site 4 the Shackleton rim, Site 7 an isolated peak near Shackleton, Site 11 the de Gerlache rim. Numbering follows Mazarico 2011 Table 3.
  - Regions of interest (RoIs) are connected pixels with more than 70% average illumination at 1 m, over 2500 m².
  - Site 1 has the most RoI area. At 5 m height the RoIs merge and nearly the entire ridge exceeds 70%.
  - At 1 m, the 1st percentile of average illumination is 67–71% across Site 1 RoIs. The 1st percentile of longest continuous illumination (LCIP) is 63–142 days, and the 99th percentile of longest continuous shadow (LCSP) is 13–22 days.
  - At 5 m, the 1st percentile of average illumination is 83–89%, LCIP is 119–231 days, and LCSP is 4–8 days.
  - Roving within an RoI (best pixel each hour, 1 m mast) cuts LCSP to 4–16 days. Adding mobility to a 5 m mast gains only about 2–5 points of illumination and 1–3 days of LCSP (to about 3–6 days).
  - Height errors are 0.30–0.50 m RMS and slope errors 1.5–2.5° RMS. Errors usually lower predicted illumination because one raised horizon pixel is enough to shadow a site.
  - The HLS requirement quoted is a 100 m-radius landing ellipse, so whole-RoI statistics are used rather than a single pixel.
  — [Barker et al., NTRS 20205009660](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf), read directly
- Point values for the 2020 lunar year (Wikipedia, citing an underlying study):

| Site | Coordinates | 2020 mean | Min per day | Max per day |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shackleton ridge A | 89.68°S, 166.0°W | 81% | 44% | 98% |
| Peak near Shackleton B | 89.44°S, 141.8°W | 82% | 56% | 100% |
| de Gerlache ridge C | 88.71°S, 68.7°W | 85% | 64% | 98% |
| Peak near Shackleton D | 88.79°S, 124.5°E | 86% | 58% | 100% |
| Malapert M1 | 86.04°S, 2.7°E | 74% | 54% | 95% |

  Also: some Shackleton rim sites are sunlit about 94% of a lunar year, and the longest joint eclipse of three Shackleton outposts is 43 h. Mast height and DEM for these points are not stated on the page. — [Wikipedia: Peak of eternal light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_of_eternal_light) (tertiary; verify against the underlying references)
- Malapert Mountain (about 122 km from the pole, Earth-facing side): one study estimates it is less than fully sunlit 11% of the time. A 2019 IAC paper says it has more than 300 days of light per year and that Earth is constantly visible from the top. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_of_eternal_light); [IAC-19 abstract](https://iafastro.directory/iac/archive/browse/IAC-19/A3/2C/53243/)
- Earth visibility is a computable output of the same LOLA horizon method, since the finite-disc source can be repositioned to the Earth. — [NASA PGDA](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69) via search summary. Libration makes some polar areas lose line of sight temporarily. — [Astronomy.com via search summary]

### Inferences
- Sites with both: Malapert massif is the clearest combined case (Earth-facing, roughly 74% mean sun at the points above). The Connecting Ridge and Shackleton rim lead on sunlight but sit at about 89.5°S, where Earth is near the horizon and libration-dependent, so DTE there needs checking or a relay. Peak near Shackleton, Haworth, Faustini, Nobile, Leibnitz Beta, Mons Mouton and de Gerlache rim cannot be ranked on numbers from this session.
- Mast height matters more than site choice at the margin: Barker's 1 m to 5 m change moves the worst-case LCSP from 13–22 days to 4–8 days at Site 1. That is a large effect (from the same table, not a separate source).
- Mean illumination (about 70–90%) is much better than the 14-day equatorial day, but the longest shadow, not the mean, drives battery size.

### Gaps
- Percent illumination, longest dark period and Earth visibility for Haworth, Faustini, Nobile rims, Leibnitz Beta, Mons Mouton, Peak near Cabeus B and Slater Plain were not found.
- No numeric Earth-visibility percentages or longest outages were found for any site. Mazarico 2011, Bussey and De Rosa 2012 and the Artemis III internal analyses are the likely sources.
- The Gläser 2014 figures, the 88.0% versus 92.1% discrepancy and the 2020 table could not be checked against full texts. I did not read the Mazarico 2011 paper itself, only its abstract.
- The Gläser PDF fetched (NTRS 20170009130) was a poster on illumination and thermal modelling with no Earth-visibility content.

## Q3. How do Sun and Earth positions behave at the pole, and which dates are favourable?

### Takeaway
The quoted geometry (Sun maximum elevation about 1.5°, Earth libration about ±6.7° latitude and ±7.9° longitude, 18.6-yr cycle) comes from the brief. I found no source for it this session. Sourced timing evidence: NASA says illumination shifts about 2–3 weeks per year, and Barker's results show seasonal and monthly patterns.

### Cited Findings
- NASA: polar illumination has strong seasonal variability and shifts about 2–3 weeks per year, so Artemis III availability depends on launch date. — [NTRS 20250011660](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250011660)
- Barker et al. show monthly and seasonal patterns across RoIs, with higher illumination in southern summer and short drops in southern summer from LDEM height uncertainty (Fig. 20). — [Barker et al.](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf)
- Gläser 2018 simulated the full 18.6-year precession cycle (2017–2037). Mazarico 2011 simulated several cycles. Both accounted for the cycle explicitly. — [DLR elib 119091](https://elib.dlr.de/119091); [NTRS 20120010094](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20120010094)
- Wikipedia's point table gives per-lunar-day minimum and maximum for 2020: Shackleton ridge A ranges 44–98% across lunar days, and Malapert M1 54–95%. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_of_eternal_light)

### Inferences
- The per-day ranges show that a monthly cycle matters (about 40 points of spread at one site). Barker finds the best illumination is in southern summer, so a landing in the local summer half of the Sun's roughly 1.5° annual declination excursion should be favoured. This is my reading of Barker's seasonal remark, not a stated rule.
- Because NASA says conditions shift by 2–3 weeks per year, a small change of launch date can swap a good window for a poor one. A few metres of mast height changes LCSP by weeks (Barker), so site shift and height are of comparable importance.

### Gaps
- No source found this session for the Sun's maximum elevation (about 1.5°), the azimuth sweep per month, Earth libration amplitudes, the ±6.7°/±7.9° values or the 18.6-yr nodal phase. These are taken from the brief and unverified.
- The current phase of the 18.6-yr cycle in 2026–2028 and the best calendar dates were not found.
- No source quantifies how fast Earth visibility changes with a small location shift.

## Q4. What are the mission-design implications?

### Takeaway
Mast height is the strongest sourced lever for power. Sources for DTE versus relay architectures were thin, with only Lunar Pathfinder schedule data and NASA's DTE-preference statement.

### Cited Findings
- Barker et al.: modelling at 1 m versus 5 m height gives very different results. A landed asset's panel height has a dramatic effect, and a 5 m mast makes errors in the topographic model matter much less. — [Barker et al.](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf)
- Mazarico 2011: about 10 m gain improves illumination substantially. Gläser 2014: 92.27% at 2 m versus 95.65% at 10 m. See Q2 for the sources.
- Artemis III planned a 5.75–6.25 day stay and a DTE option, with NRHO as the operations orbit. — [NTRS 20250011660](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250011660)
- Lunar Pathfinder (SSTL, ESA Moonlight's first node) is the first dedicated commercial lunar comms relay. It rides on Firefly's Blue Ghost 2. SSTL lists launch in 2027; some pages still say 2026. Blue Ghost 2 is now later in 2027 per Aerospace America. — [SSTL](https://www.sstl.co.uk/space-portfolio/missions-in-build/2022/lunar-pathfinder); [Aerospace America](https://aerospaceamerica.aiaa.org/nasa-expects-one-lunar-launch-this-year-moon-base-manager-says/)
- Mobility: roving within an RoI cuts the 99th-percentile shadow to 4–16 days at 1 m (see Q2). The IM-2 mission showed a south-pole lander can fail on touchdown regardless of site quality (Q1).

### Inferences
- A lander targeting about 89.5°S on the ridge with a 5 m mast faces shadow of about 4–8 days at worst, so a rechargeable-battery design can survive the longest nights only with large storage or a hibernation mode. Thermal survival through the longest shadow remains the binding constraint for small landers; I found no sourced thermal figures.
- Outside the ridge, the 14-day or 29.5-day day structure applies more weakly because the Sun skims the horizon, but I did not find sourced figures for it.
- A site on the Earth-facing side such as Malapert allows DTE with more margin, while a Shackleton-ridge lander would likely need a relay. Neither claim is sourced numerically.
- Relay assets for the pole are not yet in service: Lunar Pathfinder slipped to 2027 or later. Queqiao-2 and Artemis comm relays were not covered.

### Gaps
- No sourced thermal survival or battery-sizing numbers.
- Nothing found on Queqiao-2 coverage of the south pole, NASA's Lunar Communications Relay and Navigation Systems, or Artemis HLS DTE antenna constraints.

## Q5. What recent (2025–2026) outcomes or schedule changes affect this?

### Takeaway
Artemis III lost its landing role (Feb 2026), Artemis II succeeded (Apr 2026), IM-2 failed after tipping (Mar 2025), and the 2026–2027 polar launches have slipped largely because of New Glenn problems. Chang'e-7 moved to 2027.

### Cited Findings
- 27 Feb 2026: Artemis III becomes a LEO docking test (target mid-2027). Artemis IV and V are the 2028 landings. SLS is standardised, with Block 1B and Block 2 dropped. — [SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/nasa-makes-a-course-correction-for-the-artemis-program/)
- Artemis II flew 1–10 Apr 2026 successfully with a CSA crew member. — [OPB/AP](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/04/10/artemis-ii-astronauts-return-from-moon-with-a-splashdown-to-close-out-a-record-breaking-lunar-voyage)
- IM-2: see Q1 (6 Mar 2025, tipped inside a crater, mission over in under a day).
- VIPER reinstated on Blue Origin (Sept 2025, late 2027), conditional on MK1's first flight. — [SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/viper-might-have-a-new-ride-to-the-moon-on-blue-origin/)
- A May 2026 New Glenn pad explosion delayed Blue Origin's lunar work to 2027. Griffin-1 is NET November 2026. See Q1 for citations. May have changed.
- Chang'e-7 delayed to 2027 (announced 23 Aug 2026). — [Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/chinas-change-7-moonshot-will-seek-water-ice-at-the-lunar-south-pole)

### Inferences
- Because the landing missions are in 2028, the sun-and-Earth conditions that matter are those of 2028 and later. Date-specific windows for Artemis IV were not found.

### Gaps
- Artemis IV region or date selections, any Artemis landing date change after April 2026, and whether Griffin-1 or Chang'e-7 have a new firm date as of 14 Nov 2026.
- No current status found for Luna-26, ispace, Chandrayaan-4 or LUPEX schedules.
